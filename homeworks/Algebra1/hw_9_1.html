Why do we factorize? We can understand a complicated object better by discovering the simpler parts from which it is built. For example,

\(60=6\cdot10=2\cdot2\cdot3\cdot5.\)

Both products represent the same number. The last goes further: its factors are prime numbers. A prime number is irreducible among positive integers: it cannot be expressed as a product of two integers both greater than \(1\).

We have a similar aim with polynomials. For instance,

\(6x^2+9x=3x(2x+3).\)

The expression has not changed, but the product on the right reveals a structure concealed in the sum. We will gradually learn to decompose polynomials into simpler factors. For this homework, use only the distributive law in reverse and regrouping. Not seeing a common monomial factor does not establish that a polynomial is irreducible.

Before beginning: Write the distributive law \(a(b+c)=ab+ac\) and read it in reverse. Work each question on paper before selecting A–F. When a question requests the greatest common monomial, a correct but unfinished extraction does not meet the requirement.
